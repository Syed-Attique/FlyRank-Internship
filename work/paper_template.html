Research note · observed, measured, directional — decision-support, not causal

Can a simple model rank pages that lose search impressions better than a rule?

A client-held-out test on one month (March 2026) of FlyRank content data

Abstract

Question: can a model rank web pages whose search impressions fell within March 2026 better than a hand-written "old but visible" rule? Case study: FlyRank writes and publishes content on clients' websites, then watches search performance; pages quietly lose ranking and clicks over time, and FlyRank already flags candidates for review with product rules such as a health score and "quick win" tags, but rules struggle once signals get numerous and tangled. Data: @@PAGES@@ pseudonymized pages from one month of the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, labelled as declining when impressions on Mar 16-31 were below Mar 1-15 (37.7% of pages). Method: logistic regression and a random forest on seven page-level features (never the product's own flags), scored on 10 clients held out entirely from training, using precision among the top 50 pages. Result: the random forest reached 0.66-0.68 precision@50 against a 0.40 base rate and 0.10 for a rule built the same way FlyRank's product rules are, while a random row split inflated it to 0.86. Use: a shortlist for a human reviewer, not a forecast or a replacement for FlyRank's existing flags, because the features and label cover the same month and the test is one split of 10 clients.

1. Introduction: the FlyRank content problem

FlyRank builds and publishes content for clients, then watches how it performs in search. A page that ranked well in January can quietly slip in March, and with a portfolio of thousands of pages across many clients, no editor can check every one by hand. FlyRank already addresses this with product rules: a health score, "quick win" tags, and similar flags that fire on thresholds someone chose by hand. These rules work and run in production, but they are not learned from data, and a flag alone cannot say which of the flagged pages to open first when review time is limited.

This paper asks a narrower, testable version of that problem: for one month of FlyRank data, can a model learned from page-level signals rank declining pages ahead of stable ones better than a rule built the same way FlyRank's own flags are built? A flag that fires on more than half of pages in the starter data (54.2%) cannot say where to start; a ranking at least tries to.

2. Data

  • Source: the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse (gated release), tables fact_content_daily_performance (partition month=2026-03, 9,841,378 daily rows, Mar 1–31) and dim_content.
  • Unit: one page. After dropping pages with missing position or click-through rate, @@PAGES@@ pages remain (37 training clients, 10 test clients, @@TESTP@@ test pages).
  • Excluded: GA4 engagement fields (only 4.2% of March rows have GA4 data), and FlyRank's own product-computed scores (health score, quick-win and similar flags) and the label's own source columns -- product flags are outputs of a decision already made, never model inputs. Client names, URLs and keywords are not in the data; hashed IDs are used only for grouping and are not shown here.
  • Two page counts: the age audit in Figure 3 uses 331,437 pages including those the model drops, so its decline rates are lower than the 37.7% model base rate.

3. Methodology

  • Label (proxy): declining = impressions Mar 16–31 lower than Mar 1–15. Any drop counts, so tiny pages are noisy.
  • Features (7): impressions, average position, CTR, content age, word count, search volume, competition — all measured over the whole month.
  • Baseline: flag pages 365+ days old with 500+ impressions, ranked by impressions.
  • Models: logistic regression; random forest (300 trees, depth 8, balanced classes, seed 42).
  • Validation: one split that keeps every client wholly in training or test; zero client overlap confirmed. Metric: precision among the top 20 and top 50, always beside the base rate.
  • Leakage checks: the label's source column removed from features (a deliberate leak pushed AUC above 0.999); product flags absent; a random split compared with the grouped split; the top feature dropped and the score re-measured.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • Not a forecast. Features and label cover the same 31 days. The result shows the model can flag pages that declined within March, not that it predicts next month.
  • Small test. 50 pages from 10 clients, one split. For 0.66 on 50 pages a simple interval is roughly 0.52–0.78, and wider once client clustering is counted. The random forest scored 0.68 and 0.66 in two runs of the same setup.
  • Proxy label. Pages with a single impression can be labelled by chance; the model's most confident errors were of this kind.
  • Scope. One month, one portfolio; seasonality is not separated from decline. Associations are observational and say nothing about whether a refresh helps.

6. Ranked recommendations

  1. Use the model's top-ranked pages as a review shortlist, with a human check first. About 1 in 3 of the top 50 did not decline, and sub-1 positions or single-impression pages need manual checks.
  2. Review CTR and snippet on well-ranked, low-CTR pages, largest impressions first. CTR falls from 1.17% at positions 1–3 to 0.20% at 21+. The rule flagged @@CTR@@ of @@TOT@@ pages (@@CTRPCT@@), so it needs ordering; sorting by impressions is an untested proposal.
  3. Do not use "old and visible" alone as a refresh trigger, and do not retire FlyRank's existing flags on this result. The rule scored 0.10 against a 0.40 base rate, but this is one proxy label on one month; it tests whether a model can beat a rule of that shape, not whether FlyRank's production flags underperform.
  4. Before relying on it, re-test with features from before the label window, on a later month. Retrain if the base rate drifts far from 0.38 or precision@50 falls clearly below 0.55.
